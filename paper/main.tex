\begin{abstract}
Repetition is the strongest signal in temporal knowledge graph (TKG)
forecasting, and every recurrence mechanism in this literature scores a
historical candidate by a quantity that rises with how often it occurred and
falls with how long ago. We show that this whole family has a provable blind
spot: whenever a distractor occurred both more often and more recently than the
answer, no such scorer can rank the answer first, however it is
parameterised. The property is defined without reference to any model, so its
extent is a statistic of the data: $41.6\%$, $29.3\%$, $26.6\%$ and $15.8\%$ of
test queries on YAGO, WIKI, ICEWS18 and GDELT. We introduce PRISM, which makes
two changes. Its recurrence intensity is an unconstrained function of thirteen
inter-arrival statistics, outside the constrained family in both of the ways
the bound allows; restricting it to the monotone form costs between $1.9$ and
$8.1$ MRR. And instead of placing recurrence and structure inside one softmax,
PRISM normalises a history expert over the candidate support and a structural
expert over the remaining entities, and mixes the two distributions; controlled
ablations attribute a further $+0.37$ to $+0.67$ MRR to this separation and
none of it to how the mixing weight is computed. Over three seeds, PRISM
reaches $91.80$, $83.49$ and $26.44$ MRR on YAGO, WIKI and GDELT, above the
strongest results reported for these benchmarks, and matches a strong recent
model on ICEWS18 that we re-ran under our own verified protocol, with higher
Hits@1 and a run-to-run standard deviation roughly nine times smaller. A stratified evaluation
shows where each approach is strong: PRISM leads by $17$ MRR on queries whose
answer has a history with the subject, and trails on queries whose answer has
none, which we identify as the main open problem.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A temporal knowledge graph records facts that hold at a time, as quadruples
$(s,r,o,t)$. Forecasting asks which object completes $(s,r,?,t)$ using only
what was observed strictly before $t$.

The dominant signal in this task is repetition. A large share of the facts to
be predicted have occurred before with the same subject and relation, and a
baseline that does nothing but rank candidates by how often and how recently
they co-occurred with $(s,r)$ is already competitive with several learned
models~\cite{historyrepeats,cygnet}. Every strong system since has contained
such a term, whether as a copy vector~\cite{cygnet}, a history
vector~\cite{regcn,tirgn}, a historical/non-historical
decomposition~\cite{cenet}, a temporal point process with a learned decay
rate~\cite{gattnhp}, or a learned rule confidence~\cite{counttrucola}.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includesvg[width=\columnwidth]{intro_example}
\caption{An illustrative forecasting query (entities, event types and dates
are illustrative, not taken from a dataset). The answer, France, recurs at a
regular interval of about thirty days and is due again; the distractor,
Turkey, occurred both more often and more recently. Every recurrence scorer
that rises with the count and falls with the elapsed time ranks Turkey first,
whatever its parameters (Proposition~\ref{prop:blocked}). The history expert
of PRISM reads the full inter-arrival pattern and is not bound by this.}
\label{fig:intro}
\end{figure}

We start from a question these designs leave open: what can such a term
\emph{not} express? Fig.~\ref{fig:intro} gives an example. Read as scorers, all of them assign a historical candidate
a value that is non-decreasing in its count and non-increasing in the time
since its last occurrence. This shared monotonicity has a consequence that is
provable in one line (Proposition~\ref{prop:blocked}): if a distractor occurred
both more often and more recently than the answer, no scorer of this kind can
rank the answer first, whatever its parameters. We call such queries
\emph{monotone-blocked}. Because the definition involves only the query and the
observed histories, the share of blocked queries can be counted before any
model is trained, and it is large.

PRISM is built around two observations. The first follows directly from the
bound: its recurrence intensity is an unconstrained function of a candidate's
full inter-arrival statistics, so it is free of the monotonicity that causes
the blind spot, while still containing the classical scorer as a special case.
The second came out of our ablations. A recurrence term and a structural
encoder are usually combined inside a single softmax over all entities. We
find that it is substantially better to normalise a \emph{history expert} over
the candidates that have a history and a \emph{structural expert} over the rest,
and to mix the two distributions --- an instance of the observation that one
softmax cannot represent a mixture of two differently-shaped
distributions~\cite{mos}. The gain comes from the separate normalisation
itself: routing the mixing weight per query adds nothing.

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A characterisation of what recurrence scorers in this literature cannot
        rank (Proposition~\ref{prop:blocked}) and a model-free diagnostic that
        measures it on any dataset.
  \item An unconstrained recurrence intensity that strictly contains the
        constrained family; restricting it costs $1.9$--$8.1$ MRR.
  \item A mixture of separately normalised history and structural experts,
        with ablations that isolate which part of it carries the gain.
  \item Results over three seeds on four benchmarks under a time-aware
        filtered protocol verified against a reference implementation, a
        strong recent model re-run under the same protocol, and a stratified
        evaluation showing where each approach succeeds.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Evolutional and structural models}
RE-GCN~\cite{regcn} evolves entity representations over a window of snapshots
with a relational GCN and a recurrent update, and is the basis of many
subsequent models. CEN~\cite{cen} learns evolutional patterns of different
lengths. DiMNet~\cite{dimnet} adds cross-time message passing at equal layer
depth and disentangles node states into active and stable factors; our
structural expert adopts both, and we claim no novelty for them. xERTE~\cite{xerte}
and TITer~\cite{titer} reason over explicit subgraphs and paths, DaeMon~\cite{daemon}
propagates a query-conditioned path memory without entity embeddings, and
TiPNN~\cite{tipnn} builds a query-aware history graph.

\paragraph{Repetition and history}
CyGNet~\cite{cygnet} introduced a copy mechanism over the historical
vocabulary of a query, TiRGN~\cite{tirgn} combines a local recurrent encoder
with a global history vector, and CENET~\cite{cenet} learns to separate
historical from non-historical answers with a contrastive objective. Gastinger
et al.~\cite{historyrepeats} showed how far a recurrency baseline alone goes, and
CountTRuCoLa~\cite{counttrucola} learns interpretable rule confidences that
combine recency and frequency. LogCL~\cite{logcl} contrasts local and global
history encoders, and CognTKE~\cite{cogntke} combines fast global and deliberate
local reasoning. GAttNHP~\cite{gattnhp} casts the task as a neural Hawkes
process. Our point-process reading of the combination rule follows the
standard treatment of superposed processes~\cite{daleyvere}.

\paragraph{Evaluation}
Results in this area are reported under several filtering conventions, and
Gastinger et al.~\cite{gastingereval} showed that the choice can change
rankings. Recent work also documents that part of the standard benchmarks can
be solved by co-occurrence statistics alone~\cite{shortcut}. We therefore
verify our protocol against a reference implementation, re-run one strong
recent model under it, and report a stratified evaluation alongside the
pooled metrics.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:prelim}

A TKG is a set of quadruples $(s,r,o,t)$; inverse quadruples
$(o,r^{-1},s,t)$ are added so that both directions are predicted. For a query
$(s,r,?,t)$ the model may use only facts with timestamps strictly before $t$.
The \emph{candidate support} $S(q)$ is the set of entities that occurred as an
object of $(s,r)$, of $s$ under any relation, or among the most frequent
objects of $r$, before $t$. For each candidate $o\in S$ we compute, from the
observed history alone, $c_o$, the number of past occurrences of $(s,r,o)$, and
$\Delta t_o$, the time since the last one.

We report MRR and Hits@$\{1,3,10\}$ under the time-aware filtered protocol, in
which only other true answers \emph{at the query timestamp} are removed from
the ranking. Ties are resolved to their average position,
$\mathrm{rank} = 1 + \#\{\text{better}\} + \tfrac12(\#\{\text{tied}\}-1)$.

\section{A Blind Spot of Monotone Recurrence}
\label{sec:blocked}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:blocked}
Let a recurrence scorer be any function $\psi(c_o, \Delta t_o)$ that is
non-decreasing in its first argument and non-increasing in its second. Let
$o^\star$ be the answer and let a distractor $o$ satisfy $c_o \ge c_{o^\star}$
and $\Delta t_o \le \Delta t_{o^\star}$. Then
$\psi(c_o, \Delta t_o) \ge \psi(c_{o^\star}, \Delta t_{o^\star})$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\psi(c_o, \Delta t_o) \ge \psi(c_{o^\star}, \Delta t_o) \ge
\psi(c_{o^\star}, \Delta t_{o^\star})$, by monotonicity in the first argument
and then in the second.
\end{proof}

The hypothesis asks only that seeing a fact more often does not lower its
score and that seeing it longer ago does not raise it. It holds for any product
$f(c)\,g(\Delta t)$ and any sum $f(c)+g(\Delta t)$ with $f$ non-decreasing and
$g$ non-increasing, and for any combination non-decreasing in both, so it
covers the recurrence terms cited above, including those whose decay rate is
learned. We call a query \emph{monotone-blocked} when such a distractor exists.
Proposition~\ref{prop:blocked} leaves exactly two ways out: a scorer that is not
monotone in $(c,\Delta t)$, or one that depends on more than these two
quantities. Section~\ref{sec:rec} takes both.

shows is how often the term that carries the strongest signal of the task is
unable to order the candidates on its own.

\section{PRISM}
\label{sec:method}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includesvg[width=\textwidth]{architecture_prism}
\caption{Overview of PRISM. \emph{Top left:} the structural expert evolves
entity states over the last $H$ snapshots with cross-time GCN layers, an
active/stable split and a GRU, and a ConvTransE decoder scores every entity
($f_{\mathrm{str}}$); an optional global context summarises each entity's
full history before $t$. \emph{Bottom left:} the history expert reads the
observed occurrences of every candidate in the support $S$, summarises them by
thirteen inter-arrival statistics and maps them through an unconstrained
network ($f_{\mathrm{rec}}$), so it is not bound by
Proposition~\ref{prop:blocked}. \emph{Right:} the entity set is partitioned
into $S$ and its complement; the structural expert is normalised over the
complement, the history expert over $S$, and the two distributions are mixed
with a single learned weight $\pi$ into one exact distribution over all
entities.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{History expert: an unconstrained recurrence intensity}
\label{sec:rec}

For a candidate $o \in S$ we summarise its observed history by a vector
$\phi_o \in \mathbb{R}^{13}$: the count, the elapsed time and the mean
inter-arrival gap of the $(s,r,o)$, $(s,\cdot,o)$ and $(r,\cdot,o)$ histories,
the dispersion of the $(s,r,o)$ gaps, its span and rate, and a has-history
indicator. They are read from a compressed sparse-row temporal index and carry
no gradient. The recurrence score is
\begin{equation}
f_{\mathrm{rec}}(o) = \mathrm{MLP}\bigl[\,\mathrm{LN}(\phi_o)\,\|\,u(r)\,\|\,
v(o)\,\|\,w(E[s])\,\bigr],
\end{equation}
with learned relation, entity and subject contexts $u$, $v$, $w$ and a trunk of
two Linear--LayerNorm--GELU blocks. Nothing constrains $f_{\mathrm{rec}}$ to
decrease in $\Delta t$ or to increase in $c$, and it reads eleven statistics
beyond those two, so it is outside the family of
Proposition~\ref{prop:blocked} in both available ways; choosing the network to
compute $\log a + p\log(1+c) - b\,\Delta t$ recovers the classical scorer.

\subsection{Structural expert}

The structural expert follows prior work~\cite{dimnet,regcn}. Each of the last
$H$ snapshots is encoded by three relational GCN layers with mean and max
aggregation~\cite{pna}, a cross-time link at equal layer depth, and a split
into active and stable factors with a drift penalty on the stable factor; a
GRU carries the active factor across snapshots, and the two factors are
recombined and gated against the initial embeddings to give the entity table
$E$. A
ConvTransE decoder~\cite{convtranse} scores every entity against
$[E[s]\,\|\,R[r]]$, giving $f_{\mathrm{str}}(o)$. Because batches are
snapshots whose sizes vary by two orders of magnitude, the decoder uses
LayerNorm rather than BatchNorm.

\subsection{Separately normalised experts}
\label{sec:mix}

The two scores live on different scales and describe different populations:
$f_{\mathrm{rec}}$ is defined only on the support, and among support candidates
the ordering is decided mostly by history, while outside the support only
structure is available. PRISM therefore normalises each expert on its own set
and mixes the results:
\begin{equation}
P(o\mid q)=
\begin{cases}
\pi\, p_S(o) & o \in S,\\[2pt]
(1-\pi)\, p_N(o) & o \notin S,
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $p_S$ is a softmax over $S$ of
$\log\bigl(e^{f_{\mathrm{str}}(o)} + e^{f_{\mathrm{rec}}(o)}\bigr)$, so
structure still informs the ordering inside the support, $p_N$ is a softmax of
$f_{\mathrm{str}}$ over the complement of $S$, and $\pi = \sigma(\rho)$ is a
single learned scalar (set to $0$ when $S$ is empty). This is an exact distribution over all entities, and
training minimises its negative log-likelihood.

A single softmax over $\log(e^{f_{\mathrm{str}}}+e^{f_{\mathrm{rec}}})$ is the
special case in which both experts share one normalisation. That model must
express, with one set of logits, both how the historical candidates compare
with one another (at most 256 of them) and how all of them compare with the
thousands of entities (7.7k to 23k on our benchmarks) that have no history at
all. The mixture removes that coupling, in the same way
a mixture of softmaxes relaxes the rank limit of a single one~\cite{mos}.
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} isolates this effect from everything else that
changes between the two.

\subsection{Global history context (optional)}
\label{sec:global}

The structural expert sees only the last $H$ snapshots, so an entity that was
quiet in that window enters the decoder with little more than its static
embedding. As an optional component we add to each entity's initial state the
mean, over all of its facts $(o,r,x,t')$ with $t'<t$, of $E_0[x]+R[r]$, passed
through a projection initialised at zero. It is computed with one chunked
scatter-add over the facts before $t$, reads nothing at or after $t$, and does
not depend on training order. Section~\ref{sec:global-res} reports where it
helps and where it does not.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Data and protocol}
We use ICEWS18, GDELT, WIKI and YAGO with their standard splits. Before
reporting any number we checked our evaluation code against the RE-GCN
reference implementation: the scored query counts are identical on all four
datasets, the splits are temporally disjoint, and the filtered answer sets
agree on $4{,}000$ sampled test queries per dataset with no differences.

\paragraph{Baselines}
Published numbers are taken from the DiMNet comparison table for ICEWS18 and
GDELT and from the DaeMon table for WIKI and YAGO, both of which use the
time-aware filtered protocol. Because tables in this area are not always
directly comparable, we additionally re-ran LogCL~\cite{logcl}, a strong recent
model, three times on ICEWS18 with its published hyperparameters and evaluated
its saved score matrices with \emph{our} evaluation code. LogCL's own evaluator
and ours give the same MRR, Hits@1, Hits@3 and Hits@10 on the same run
($35.41$ / $24.09$ / $40.17$ / $57.95$),
which confirms that the two measure the same quantity.

\paragraph{Training}
$d = 200$, three GCN layers, history of ten snapshots (five on GDELT), Adam
with learning rate $10^{-3}$, label smoothing $0.1$, early stopping on
validation MRR, three seeds per configuration. Candidate supports and their
statistics depend only on the query and on earlier facts, so they are computed
once per dataset and served from a shared memory-mapped cache; this makes an
epoch $1.5$--$24\times$ faster (GDELT: from $25$--$50$ to about $2$ minutes on
one A100) and leaves every input tensor bit-identical. Parameter counts are
$6.0$M on YAGO and $8.6$M on ICEWS18, dominated by the entity table.

Moving from a single softmax to separately normalised experts changes several
things at once, so Table~\ref{tab:ablation} separates them. The single-softmax
model was trained with an auxiliary cross-entropy on the structural scores,
which PRISM does not use, and without the subject context in the history
expert. Matching both settings (row~3) recovers almost none of the gap: PRISM
remains $+0.37$ MRR ahead on WIKI and $+0.67$ on ICEWS18, four to six times the
seed standard deviation. Within the separately normalised family, how the
mixing weight is computed does not matter: a query-dependent router is no
better than a single scalar, and letting the structural expert's distribution
cover the support as well (so that the likelihood no longer factorises) gives
the same result. What carries the gain is that the history candidates are
normalised among themselves rather than against every entity in the graph.
We therefore use the simplest form, a constant $\pi$.

\subsection{The monotone restriction}

Restricting the history expert to exactly the published form,
$\log a + p\log(1+c) - b\,\Delta t$ with $p,b\ge 0$ predicted from $(r,o)$, and
holding everything else fixed lowers MRR from $91.72$ to $89.81$ on YAGO, from
$82.94$ to $74.83$ on WIKI, from $34.77$ to $29.20$ on ICEWS18 and from $26.33$
to $21.77$ on GDELT (single-softmax model in both cases; restricted variant one
seed). A unit test sweeps $\Delta t$ and $c$ to confirm that the restricted
expert is monotone and the full one is not. On YAGO and WIKI the loss
concentrates on the blocked stratum ($-5.30$ and $-18.61$ Hits@1, against
$-1.98$ and $-5.95$ on unblocked queries with a history), as
Proposition~\ref{prop:blocked} predicts. On ICEWS18 and GDELT the restriction
costs more on unblocked queries ($-17.44$ and $-16.03$ Hits@1) than on blocked
ones ($-2.70$ and $-1.26$): there the additional statistics matter beyond the
blocked configuration.

Pooled metrics hide how differently two models can reach similar numbers. We
partition the ICEWS18 test set into three disjoint strata --- \emph{clean}
(the answer has a history and is not dominated), \emph{blocked}, and
\emph{no history} (the answer never occurred with $(s,r)$) --- and rank LogCL's
saved scores with the same code (Table~\ref{tab:strat}). Wherever the answer
has a history with the subject, PRISM is far ahead: $+17.1$ MRR on clean
queries, and higher Hits@10 on blocked ones. The whole deficit comes from the
half of the queries whose answer has no history, where LogCL's global history
encoder reaches $20.5$ MRR and PRISM $12.7$. Weighted by the stratum shares,
these two effects nearly cancel, which is why the pooled MRRs are close and why
LogCL leads on Hits@10. The two approaches are complementary, and the
no-history stratum is the clearest direction for improving PRISM.

\subsection{Global history context}
\label{sec:global-res}

The optional global context of Section~\ref{sec:global} targets exactly that
stratum. On ICEWS18 it raises MRR from $35.23$ to $35.43$ and Hits@10 from
$54.36$ to $54.96$ over three seeds, with the gain on no-history queries
(Hits@10 $25.43 \to 26.08$). It leaves YAGO and GDELT unchanged and lowers WIKI
by $0.18$, where nearly all answers have a history; a second hop of
aggregation adds nothing further. It narrows the no-history gap but does not
close it, and we do not include it in the default configuration.

%% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Negative Results}
\label{sec:negative}

We report designs that did not work, since each rules out an explanation.
\emph{An auxiliary structural loss} hurt on YAGO ($-0.87$ MRR, three seeds) and
raised seed variance. \emph{A path-based third expert} in the style of DaeMon
doubled Hits@1 on the no-history stratum of YAGO but lowered overall MRR, at
roughly fifty times the cost of the other branches. \emph{Conditioning the
history expert on the subject} was intended to let it step back on
no-history queries; it changed that stratum by at most $0.2$ Hits@1. And
extending the support with \emph{two-hop candidates} was ruled out before
building it: two-hop sets are large (median $4{,}098$ entities on ICEWS18), and
ranking them by path count places fewer no-history answers in the top $64$
($12\%$) than PRISM already places in its top $10$ ($25\%$).

%% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Limitations}

The no-history stratum remains the main open problem on event datasets. On
ICEWS18 our model is on par with, not ahead of, the strongest model we re-ran,
and behind it on Hits@10. We re-ran one recent model rather than all of them.
All four benchmarks are known to be partly solvable by co-occurrence
statistics~\cite{shortcut}; our stratified evaluation is a response to this,
not a solution, and the bias-corrected datasets proposed there are a natural
next test.

%% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Conclusion}

Recurrence scorers that are monotone in count and elapsed time cannot order a
measurable share of forecasting queries, whatever their parameters. An
unconstrained recurrence intensity removes that limit, and normalising history
and structure as separate experts, rather than inside one softmax, adds a
consistent further gain that our ablations attribute to the separation itself.
The resulting model is strongest exactly where a history exists, and the
stratified comparison points to the queries without one as the place where the
next improvement must come from.
